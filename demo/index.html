How our sonar sees

A speaker clicks, a microphone listens, and the time in between tells us how far away things are. A servo turns the sensor so we can scan a whole arc, and Python draws what it finds like a submarine's sonar screen.

From a click of sound to a dot on the screen

1 Sensor

Ping

The Arduino pulses the Trig pin for 10 µs. The sensor fires 8 bursts of 40 kHz sound, too high for us to hear.

2 Sensor

Echo

The sound bounces off an object and comes back. The Echo pin stays HIGH for exactly as long as the round trip took.

3 Arduino

Calculate

pulseIn() measures that time in microseconds. Dividing by 58 turns it into centimetres.

4 USB cable

Send

The Arduino prints one line of text, like 90,123: the angle, a comma, then the distance. -1 means no echo.

5 Python

Draw

Python reads each line, turns angle and distance into an x,y point, and draws a glowing dot. Dots close together are grouped into one object.

↻ Then repeat. The servo turns 2°, waits 30 ms to settle, and pings again. It sweeps from 15° to 165° and back, about 13 pings a second.
The physics and the maths

Where does "divide by 58" come from?

Speed of sound343 m/s
Sound travels there and back200 cm
Echo pin HIGH for5,831 µs
Arduino works out (time ÷ 58)100 cm
Line sent over USB90,100

Sound covers 1 cm in 1 ÷ 0.0343 = 29.15 µs. It goes there and back, so each centimetre of distance costs 2 × 29.15 ≈ 58 µs.

Turning angle + distance into a dot

The sensor gives us polar coordinates (an angle and a distance). The screen needs x and y. Trigonometry converts between them.

On a screen, y counts downwards, so the code uses y = centre − r·sin θ to draw upwards.

Think about it

Questions to try

Why does a cushion or a jumper barely show up, but a book shows up clearly?

Soft, fuzzy materials absorb sound instead of reflecting it, so too little comes back. Hard, flat surfaces facing the sensor reflect most of it straight back.

Why does the code wait between pings instead of firing as fast as possible?

An echo from a far wall could arrive after the next ping has gone out, and the sensor would mistake it for a close object. Waiting lets old echoes die away. The servo also needs time to stop moving.

A real submarine uses sonar in water. What would change in our maths?

Sound travels about 1,480 m/s in water, over 4 times faster than in air, so the echo comes back much sooner. "Divide by 58" would become roughly "divide by 13.5". Tick the underwater box above to see it.

Two objects are side by side, 10 cm apart. Why might the sonar show them as one blob?

The sensor's sound beam spreads out in a cone about 15° wide, so it can hear both at once. Further away, the cone is wider, so objects need to be further apart to be told apart. That is the sonar's resolution.

The temperature in the room rises from 10 °C to 30 °C. Does that matter?

Yes, a little. Sound travels faster in warm air (about 337 m/s at 10 °C and 349 m/s at 30 °C), so the same object would read about 3% closer on a hot day.
